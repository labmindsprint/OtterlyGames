Learning path · Telling time

Two hands. One time. Plenty of room to explore.

Read the hour first, then find how far through it you are. Start with what the learner can recognise, and add minutes when hours feel familiar.

Before you begin

Choose a starting step

Before hours

Check that the learner can find numbers 1–12 around a clock face and distinguish the short hand from the long hand. Trace the numbers in order; begin at 12 and go clockwise.

Before five-minute times

Practise counting 5, 10, 15, …, 60. If that is new, keep the long hand at 12 while exploring hours. Counting in fives is a next step, not an entry test.

Worked example

Worked example: why 3:30 is “half past three”

Read the short hand first

At 3:00, the short hand points to 3 and the long hand points to 12. Move the long hand halfway round to 6. Thirty minutes have passed, so the short hand is now halfway between 3 and 4.

The hour is still 3: it has passed 3 but has not reached 4. The short hand does not wait at 3 and jump at the end.

Count the minutes

Starting just after 12, count five minutes at each number: 1 is 5, 2 is 10, 3 is 15, 4 is 20, 5 is 25, and 6 is 30. Say “three thirty” or “half past three”.

Check with a second example: at 3:45, the long hand is at 9 and the short hand is closer to 4, but the written hour is still 3. This is “quarter to four”.

A clock face alone cannot tell you AM or PM. The same hand positions can mean morning or evening. Use a familiar event, such as breakfast or bedtime, to supply that context.

Practice

Try a short, untimed session

  1. Explore: use Hour Hand to set 2, 7 and 12 o'clock. Ask which hand gives the hour.
  2. Connect: open Minute Hand. Count in fives to the 3 and then the 6. Ask how many minutes make a full turn.
  3. Read: in Clock Lessons, choose Hours Only or Half Past. Try three questions and explain one answer; there is no need to keep a streak.
  4. Leave the screen: draw a clock or look at a real one. Ask “Where will the short hand be in half an hour?” Return another day if that question is a stretch.

Around five minutes is enough for a first look, but this is not a countdown. Stop sooner, repeat a step or spend longer on one clock if that suits the learner.

When it is tricky

If this is tricky, change the question

“The 6 means six minutes”

Put a mark for each five-minute group around a drawn face. Count six groups of five to reach 30. The printed numbers name hours; the long hand uses them as minute markers.

“It is nearly 4, so the hour is 4”

Start at 3:00 and move forward a little at a time. Ask which hour has already begun. Compare 3:55 with 4:00 to see the boundary.
