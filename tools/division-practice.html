How to Learn Division Facts

Division is essentially multiplication in reverse. If your child knows that 6 × 7 = 42, then they also know that 42 ÷ 7 = 6. Building division fluency therefore starts with strong times-table knowledge.

Use Study mode to explore a divisor, then try typed answers in Practice. Start with familiar facts and use the division learning path to connect them to sharing and grouping activities. A parent-friendly walkthrough is in division for kids, explained simply.

Why No Remainders?

This tool focuses on exact division facts (“how many 3s in 12?”) because these form the building blocks your child needs before tackling division with remainders or long division.

Once exact division facts feel automatic, try our Long Division tool for step-by-step bracket division with remainders.

Tips for Parents

Keep sessions manageable and pause if your child is frustrated. Ask how they found an answer rather than focusing only on a score. A paper division sheet is another format to try together.

Use sharing in everyday life to reinforce division: sharing fruit equally, splitting a pack of stickers, or dividing a pizza. Real-world connections make abstract maths meaningful.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you teach division to a child?

Start by connecting division to sharing: 'If you have 12 sweets and 3 friends, how many does each person get?' Use objects to make it concrete. Once the concept clicks, introduce division as the reverse of multiplication: if 4 × 3 = 12, then 12 ÷ 3 = 4. Practice one divisor at a time.

What division facts should kids know?

This tool covers divisors from 2 to 12 with answers up to 12, so dividends go up to 144. These mirror the times tables in reverse. Many families start with ÷2, ÷5 and ÷10, then move through the others in whatever order suits the child. Curricula vary, so check what your child's school expects.

Why can division feel harder than multiplication?

Many children find division harder because it works backwards from a product and needs the matching multiplication fact. Regular, low-pressure practice with the times tables can help build that reverse recall.
