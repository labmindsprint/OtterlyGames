Spelling Games vs Spelling Practice: What Helps?

What each is good for, where each falls short, and how to combine them in a short routine at home.

In this article

  1. What each one is good for
  2. Pick a few words, not a long list
  3. Cover, write, check
  4. Using a spelling game well
  5. A five-minute routine
  6. Common mistakes

Spelling games and spelling practice are not rivals, but they do different jobs. A game can make a child willing to start, while a slower practice routine helps them look closely at the parts of a word. Most children benefit from a little of both, and this guide shows how to put them together.

We are a game studio, so we have a view on games. We will try to be fair about it: neither format is magic, and what suits your child depends on their age, what they are learning at school and how they feel about spelling.

What each one is good for

Spelling gamesSpelling practice
Good forGetting started, keeping interest, hearing words read aloudNoticing word parts, fixing one tricky spelling, writing by hand
Watch out forGuessing quickly, chasing streaks, rushing through wordsLong lists, copying without thinking, feeling like a test
Works best whenAn adult looks at the mistakes afterwardsIt is short and the words are chosen with care

Pick a few words, not a long list

Two or three words with one useful feature are easier to learn than ten unrelated hard ones. Choose words from a book your child is reading or from their school list, and follow the phonics or spelling order their teacher uses where you can. Before you start, check that your child knows what each word means. Ask them to use it in a sentence.

Cover, write, check

This simple paper routine is the heart of practice. It works with any word.

Worked example: ship

Say it. "The ship sailed away." Say the word slowly: sh · i · p.

Notice. "Ship" has four letters but three sounds, because the letters s and h work together as one sound. Write sh | i | p in three spaces.

Cover and write. Look closely, cover the word and write it from memory.

Check. Uncover the word and compare one part at a time. If your child wrote "sip", point to the sh and say its sound together. Try once more.

Not every English spelling follows a simple sound-by-sound rule. For a word like "said", notice the unexpected ai together, as well as the parts that are familiar.

Using a spelling game well

A game is most useful as a warm-up or a change of pace. With Spelling Bee, your child listens to a word, reads a hint and types the spelling. It has Easy, Medium and Hard levels, and you can start with comfortable words and change the level when your child is ready. The streak counter is feedback, not a target to beat.

A few practical points:

  • The tool reads words aloud using your browser's built-in speech, which varies between devices. Test the sound and volume before you hand over the device.
  • If speech is not available or sound is not suitable, use the paper routine with an adult reading the words.
  • Sit with your child for the first few words and look at any mistakes together.
  • Keep personal details such as names and addresses out of any practice words.

A five-minute routine

  1. Choose (1 minute). Pick two or three words and talk about one spelling feature in each.
  2. Play a little (2 minutes). Try a few words in the Spelling Bee game, or have an adult read them aloud.
  3. Cover, write, check (1 minute). Take one word that was tricky and use the paper routine above.
  4. Find it (1 minute). Leave the screen and look for one of the words in a book, label or handwritten note.

You can stop after one word. There is no need to extend a streak or move to a harder level.

Common mistakes

  • Letting the game replace the thinking. If your child is guessing, slow down and say the sounds out loud.
  • Practising a long list at once. A short session with fewer words is easier to remember than a marathon.
  • Only marking wrong. Point out the part they got right, then focus on the one part to revisit.
  • Skipping meaning. A word that your child can say but not explain is harder to remember.
  • Treating level labels as age tests. They are a starting point. Your child's teacher knows what is being taught.
