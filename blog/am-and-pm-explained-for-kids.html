AM and PM Explained: A Simple Guide for Kids

Why the clock goes around twice a day, how noon and midnight work, and simple ways to practise together.

AM and PM can feel confusing because the same clock time happens twice a day. 3 o'clock arrives in the afternoon and again in the middle of the night, and the clock face looks identical both times. This guide explains AM and PM for kids in plain language, with examples and a short routine you can try at home.

It works best once your child can read the hands on an analogue clock. If they are still learning that, our guide to practising telling time at home is a good place to start.

The short version

Here is the idea in two lines you can say out loud:

AM is midnight to noon. PM is noon to midnight.

Each half lasts 12 hours. The day has two of them, which is why the clock goes around twice. The table shows a few examples. Daily routines vary between families, so treat the "usually" column as a conversation starter rather than a rule.

Why the clock goes around twice

A day has 24 hours, but an ordinary clock face only shows 12 numbers. So the short hand travels around the clock once from midnight to noon, and again from noon to midnight. AM and PM tell you which trip you are on.

A picture helps. Draw a line across a sheet of paper with midnight at the left, noon in the middle and midnight again at the right. Mark breakfast, lunch, dinner and bedtime together. Your child can see that "7 o'clock" appears once on each side of noon.

What AM and PM stand for

AM stands for "ante meridiem", Latin for "before midday". PM stands for "post meridiem", "after midday". Your child does not need the Latin, but "AM starts just after midnight and PM starts at noon" is a handy way to remember the order.

Why 12 is the tricky number

The twelve o'clock times break the pattern children expect. By convention, noon is written 12:00 PM and midnight is written 12:00 AM. That feels backwards, because 12 AM sounds like it should be the middle of the day.

A tip for parents

Introduce the 12 o'clock exceptions after your child is comfortable with the basic idea. When you write a schedule, you can also write "noon" or "midnight" in words to avoid any confusion.

A five-minute routine

  1. Draw the day (2 minutes). Make the midnight to noon to midnight line from above and add three or four events your child chooses.
  2. Play "AM or PM?" (2 minutes). Name an activity, such as eating breakfast, watching the stars or playing at the park, and ask which half of the day it usually belongs to. Talk about exceptions: some families eat dinner early or have breakfast late.
  3. Check with the slider (1 minute). Open the AM and PM tool, slide through the day and watch the sky and activities change. Ask your child to stop it at a time they know.

Common mix-ups

  • "AM means morning and PM means night." Close, but PM includes the afternoon, and AM includes the dark hours after midnight. Using "midnight to noon" and "noon to midnight" is more precise.
  • "12:30 AM is lunchtime." It is half an hour after midnight. Draw it on the day line to see why.
  • "7 o'clock is always the morning." It also comes round in the evening. The clock face cannot tell you which, so look for a clue such as a bedtime story or breakfast.

What comes next: the 24-hour clock

Once AM and PM feel familiar, you can show how the afternoon hours continue counting past 12: 1 PM is 13:00, 2 PM is 14:00, and so on. Many digital devices show time this way, so it is a natural next step. There is no hurry, and your child's school may introduce it at a different point.
