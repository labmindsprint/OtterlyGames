What's the Best Way to Practise Telling Time at Home?

Short, calm routines that connect the clock to your child's day, with a five-minute plan you can repeat.

In this article

  1. Little and often, one idea at a time
  2. Put a real clock where your child can see it
  3. A five-minute routine you can repeat
  4. Everyday moments that count as practice
  5. A worked example: reading 4:35

The best way to practise telling time at home is usually small and regular: a few calm minutes with a real clock your child can see, tied to things that already happen in your day. No single method suits every child, so treat this guide as a menu rather than a programme.

It assumes your child can already find the numbers 1 to 12 and tell the short hand from the long hand. If not, begin with our step-by-step guide to teaching a child to tell time, then come back here for practice routines.

Little and often, one idea at a time

Clock reading has several layers: the hour hand, the minute hand, counting in fives, and phrases such as "half past" and "quarter to". Practising all of them at once is a common reason it feels stuck. Choose one layer, practise it for a few days, and add the next only when the first feels easy.

A sensible order is whole hours, half past, quarter past and quarter to, five-minute steps, then single minutes. Your child's school may use a different order, so ask their teacher what is being covered and follow that where it differs.

Put a real clock where your child can see it

Practice sticks better when the clock is part of the room rather than a task on the table.

  • Hang an analogue clock in the kitchen or bedroom, at your child's eye level if you can.
  • Make a paper plate clock with two card hands and a split pin, so your child can set times with their own hands.
  • Read the hands first, then check on a digital clock or phone. That keeps the analogue clock doing the thinking.

A five-minute routine you can repeat

This is a suggestion, not a rule. Stop sooner if your child is tired, and repeat the same step on another day if it needs it.

  1. Look (1 minute). Glance at the real clock together. Ask which hand tells the hour and which counts the minutes.
  2. Set it (1 minute). Say a time such as "7 o'clock" or "half past 3" and let your child set it on the paper plate clock.
  3. Read it (1 minute). Swap roles. You set the hands, and your child says the time. Make one mistake on purpose and see whether they spot it.
  4. Try a tool (1 minute). Open Clock Lessons, pick one of the five levels yourself, and answer three questions together. Ask your child to explain one answer out loud.
  5. Link it to the day (1 minute). Ask, "What do we usually do at this time?" Connecting the clock to breakfast, school or bedtime gives the numbers a meaning.

Everyday moments that count as practice

You do not need extra sessions for these. They fit into what you are already doing:

  • "Bedtime is at half past seven. Where will the long hand be pointing?"
  • "We leave for school at quarter to eight. Can you show me on the clock?"
  • "It is 5:15 now and dinner is in 30 minutes. What will the clock say?"
  • Let your child be the family time-keeper for one routine, such as getting shoes on.

Keep it light

One question at a time is plenty. If it starts to feel like a test, put the clock away and try again tomorrow.

A worked example: reading 4:35

Say it out loud together

1. Hour first. The short hand has passed the 4 but has not reached the 5, so the hour is 4.

2. Count the minutes. The long hand points to the 7. Count in fives from the 12: 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35. That is 35 minutes.

3. Say it. "Four thirty-five." Later you can add "twenty-five to five".

A quick check: 35 minutes is a little more than half an hour, so the short hand should sit just past the halfway point between the 4 and the 5. If it does not, look again at which hand is which.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Reading the nearest hour. At 4:50 the short hand looks close to 5, but it is still 4:50. The hour is the number the short hand has passed.
  • Reading the 7 as seven minutes. The numbers are minute markers in groups of five. Practise counting in fives before combining it with the clock.
  • Mixing up the hands. Colour the short hand on a paper clock, or say "short is the hour" each time.
  • Practising too long. Several short turns beat one long, tiring one.

When to move on

Move to the next layer when your child can read the current one on a few different days without hints, even if they are a little slow. Stay longer if they are guessing or getting upset. Telling time takes many children months, and your child's teacher is the best guide to what they need next.
