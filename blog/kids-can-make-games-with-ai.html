Your Kid Can Build a Game with AI — Here's How

An adult-led introduction to game making: explain HTML and Git, check provider age rules, and review AI-generated code together before running or sharing it.

Adults: Read This Before Opening an AI Tool

This is an adult-led activity, not a recommendation for young children to create AI or GitHub accounts. Stay involved throughout. Check each provider's current minimum age, parental-consent rules, permitted supervised use, privacy terms, and regional requirements before using it. An adult account is not a way around an age restriction.

Start with the current GitHub terms, OpenAI terms, or Anthropic terms; check Manus's current terms too if considering that service. If the proposed use is not permitted, keep the activity offline with paper and a local editor.

Do not put children's names, school, location, photos, voices, passwords, or other personal information into prompts or project files. An adult must review generated text, images, code, external connections, and permissions before running it or showing it to a child. Do not publish a repository or game until an adult has reviewed it and explicitly chosen to share it.

My 8-year-old finished playing Dog Shepherd — our silly herding game — and said: "Mama, I want to give super powers to dogs. Wolves are powerful. Why can't the dog have powers too?"

That's not just a game idea. That's a product idea. He saw a mechanic, imagined a twist, and wanted to build it. He didn't know it, but he was thinking like a product designer.

Here's the thing parents don't hear often enough: your kid doesn't have to just play games — they can make them. And thanks to AI, the barrier to entry has never been lower. This blog is a practical, step-by-step guide to help you get started — tonight, at your kitchen table.

Why Playing Games Builds the Foundation

Before we talk about building, let's talk about why playing matters more than most parents think.

When your child plays a stealth game like Zombie Hide & Seek, they're not just having fun. They're learning:

  • Cause and effect — "If I move here, the zombie sees me"
  • Systems thinking — "The flashlight reveals enemies but also reveals me"
  • Optimization — "What's the fastest safe path?"
  • Failure resilience — "I died. Let me try a different strategy."

Playing together can be one source of design ideas. Drawing a board game or inventing a playground game is another; a child does not need more screen time before they can begin making things.

The Product-Builder Mindset

There's a difference between a consumer mindset and a builder mindset:

Consumer Mindset Builder Mindset
"This game is fun" "What makes this game fun?"
"I want more levels" "How would I design a new level?"
"This is too hard" "How could I make this more balanced?"
"I wish this existed" "I can build this myself"

These questions offer a way to explore design if your child is interested. Enjoying a game and making one are both valid activities; every play session does not need to become a project.

First, Let's Understand the Big Words

Before your child can build a game, they need to understand a few ideas. Here's how I explained them to my 8-year-old — using things he already knows.

What Is the Internet?

Think of the internet like a giant post office. When you type otterlygames.com in your browser, you're sending a letter that says: "Can I see this website, please?" That letter zooms through cables and towers until it reaches the right building. That building sends back the website, and your browser shows it on the screen.

Every website lives on a computer somewhere in the world. The internet is just the roads that connect your computer to all those other computers.

What Is a Server? What Is a Client?

Server = the library. It has all the books (websites, games, data). It waits for people to ask for things.

Client = you walking into the library. You ask for a book, the librarian (server) gives it to you, and you read it at your table (your browser).

When you open a game on your phone, your phone is the client. The computer that stores the game and sends it to you is the server.

What Is HTML?

HTML is the language that every web page speaks. It's like LEGO instructions — it tells the browser: "Put a heading here. Put a picture there. Make a button that says START."

A small browser game can live in one HTML file, with CSS for appearance and JavaScript for behaviour. Larger games often need more files. A browser still downloads or opens code; no app-store installation does not mean there is nothing to review.

Here's what it looks like:

<h1>My Dog Game</h1>
<p>The dog has super powers!</p>
<button>START GAME</button>

That's real code. It's not scary. It's just English words inside angle brackets.

What Is Git? (And Commit? And Push?)

Imagine you're writing a story in a notebook. Every few pages, you take a photo of your notebook — so if you spill juice on it, you can look at the photo and rewrite it.

Git is that camera. It takes snapshots of your project every time you want to save your progress.

  • Commit = taking the photo. You say, "Save what I have right now." You also write a small note like: "Added the dog's laser power."
  • Push = sending committed snapshots to a chosen remote repository, such as one on GitHub. An adult must review the files and visibility settings first. Public repositories can be seen by others; a remote copy is not a promise that something is saved forever.
  • Pull = getting the latest photos from the safe, in case someone else made changes.

So: write code → commit (save snapshot) → push (send to the cloud). That's it. That's what professional developers do every day.

The Tools: What You Actually Need

You can begin with paper and a local text editor. VS Code is free; AI services may have usage limits, paid plans, accounts, and eligibility rules. Check the current terms before choosing one. No cloud service is required to sketch or edit a simple local game.

VS Code — A Shared Local Workshop

VS Code (Visual Studio Code) is a free app from Microsoft where you write code. Think of it as Google Docs — but for code instead of essays.

It does some cool things:

  • Colors your code so it's easy to read (like syntax highlighting in a storybook)
  • Tells you when you made a typo (red squiggly lines — just like Word!)
  • Has a built-in "terminal" — a text box where you can talk to your computer directly
  • Works on Windows, Mac, and Chromebook

Parent tip: Download VS Code and open it. Create a file called game.html. That's step one.

GitHub Copilot — The AI That Writes Code With You

GitHub Copilot can suggest code inside an editor. It can also make mistakes. Only use the service where the account holder and the proposed activity meet the provider's current requirements.

An eligible adult can turn a child's paper idea into a prompt such as:

// Make a game where a dog runs across the screen
// and the player clicks to give it super powers
// wolves chase the dog but the powers defeat them

Copilot may suggest code to try. An adult should read it before accepting or running it, check for unwanted remote scripts or requests, and test it separately before involving a child. Changes such as "Make the wolves bigger" may need several attempts.

Check current plan limits and eligibility. Do not create an underage GitHub account for this activity or ask a child to accept generated code without adult review.

Manus / ChatGPT / Claude — The Idea-to-Game Machine

An eligible adult may consider a browser-based service such as Manus, ChatGPT, or Claude, but only where that provider permits the intended activity. These are not recommendations for young children to use a chatbot independently. Keep the child-led part on paper if provider rules do not allow participation.

"Build me a game in one HTML file. A dog with super powers fights wolves. Arrow keys to move. Space bar to shoot a laser beam. The dog has 3 lives. If a wolf touches the dog, it loses a life. Make it colorful."

The AI may produce an HTML file, but it may be incomplete or unsuitable. Ask for a self-contained local game with no remote scripts, forms, accounts, purchases, or tracking, and have an adult verify that it actually meets those requirements before opening it. If you cannot review the code, ask an experienced adult for help or use the paper design activity instead.

Your child can suggest changes for the adult to evaluate, such as:

  • "Add a shield power-up"
  • "Make the wolves come in waves"
  • "The dog should bark when it shoots"
  • "Add a score counter"

Each suggestion is a design decision. The adult remains responsible for the service use, the code, and what is shown or shared.

A Practical Project: Start Small and Take Your Time

The timings below are optional planning ideas, not a completion promise. Setup, code review, and debugging can take much longer. It is fine to finish with a drawing or a local prototype instead of a published game.

Step 1: Play Together First (5 min)

Choose an age-appropriate game to explore together, perhaps one from our Otterly Arcade, or use a board game you already know. As you play, ask your child:

  • "What's your favorite part?"
  • "What would you change?"
  • "What game do YOU wish existed?"

Step 2: Write the Game Idea (5 min)

Help your child write down their game idea. Keep it simple — a few sentences is perfect:

Example Game Idea:

Name: Super Dog
What happens: A dog with laser eyes fights waves of wolves
Controls: Arrow keys to move, space to shoot
How you win: Survive 5 waves
Power-up: Bones give you a shield for 5 seconds

Congrats — your child just wrote a product spec. That's what software teams do every day.

Step 3: Turn It Into a Game (10 min)

Option A — Adult-operated AI, where permitted: The eligible adult sends a non-identifying game idea to a chosen service. Ask for a self-contained HTML file, review the output and any external connections, and test it before showing the result to a child. Do not paste code into a terminal or open an unreviewed download.

Option B — Local editor: Make a new project folder and a file called super-dog.html in VS Code. Begin with the heading and button example above, then add one small behaviour at a time. Copilot is optional and subject to the same account and adult-review requirements. A simple local file does not need a public website or an extension.

Step 4: Iterate and Improve (10 min)

This is where the real learning happens. Your child plays their game and says:

  • "The wolves are too slow" → Tell the AI to speed them up
  • "I want the dog to have a different power each level" → Tell the AI
  • "Can I add sound when the dog barks?" → Tell the AI
  • "It's too easy" → Tell the AI to add more wolves per wave

This is a chance to practise a build → test → learn loop. An adult should review each change before running it again; a new AI edit can add unwanted code as well as the feature you requested.

Step 5: Save It with Git (5 min — optional but powerful)

If your child is curious, an adult can explain local Git snapshots. Git itself does not require a GitHub account. In a new project folder containing only files you have reviewed:

  1. An adult opens the terminal in that project folder and runs git init.
  2. Review git status, then stage only the intended file with git add super-dog.html.
  3. Review the staged file with git diff --cached before using git commit -m "My first dog game!". Remember that commit identity settings may include an adult's name and email.
  4. Stop here for a local project. A push is optional, requires an eligible account, and is not the same as publishing a playable website.

Before any upload or publication, an adult should check files and commit history for personal information, credentials, copied assets, and unwanted connections. Choose visibility deliberately, obtain any needed permissions, and review the actual shared page. Keeping the project local is a complete and worthwhile outcome.

Choose a Starting Point by Readiness and Eligibility

Starting Point Activity What to Expect
New to game design Paper, counters, and drawings Describe a goal and invent a rule together. No cloud account or chatbot is needed.
Ready to explore local files A local editor with an adult Change a heading, colour, or rule in a reviewed local file. Explain snapshots without requiring a GitHub account.
Eligible for a chosen service Provider-approved use with adult oversight Check the exact provider's age, consent, and regional rules first. Review output before use and keep publication a separate adult decision.

What Kids Actually Learn

A small, adult-led project can offer chances to practise ideas beyond coding:

  • Creative thinking — Imagining something that doesn't exist yet
  • Problem decomposition — Breaking a big idea into small, buildable pieces
  • Clear communication — Describing ideas so precisely that a machine can understand them
  • Critical thinking — "This isn't fun. Why? How do I fix it?"
  • Persistence — Debugging and improving until it feels right
  • Empathy — "Would my friend enjoy this? What would they find confusing?"

These are the skills that matter in every career — not just tech.

From Player to Builder

Our Otterly Arcade offers small game ideas to explore together. Pick one rule to talk about, then imagine how changing it would affect the game.

If your child wants to make something, try one manageable idea together. Drawing a character or writing a rule is a useful start; there is no requirement to open a cloud tool or publish a result.

So tonight, sit down and ask: "What game do you wish existed?"

Then sketch a rule, make a paper prototype, or open a reviewed local file together. Use an AI service only if its rules permit the activity, and keep adult supervision in place. There is no deadline and no expectation that a young child should do this alone.

And that's the whole point.
