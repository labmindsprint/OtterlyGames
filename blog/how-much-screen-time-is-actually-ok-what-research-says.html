How Much Screen Time Is Actually OK? A Family Guide

A practical family guide to choosing content, planning breaks, and balancing screens with everyday life. Not medical advice or a universal daily allowance.

Choosing when to use a screen can be a surprisingly busy part of family life. A video call, a favourite story, a learning game, and homework all use a screen, but they serve different purposes. It helps to talk about the activity as well as the clock.

About this guide: We make Clock Master and Math Tank. These are practical planning ideas from a game-making parent, not medical advice, a research review, or evidence that our games improve health or learning outcomes.

Is There One Daily Number That Fits Every Family?

This guide does not prescribe a universal daily allowance. Consider your child's age, needs, content, total screen use, sleep, movement, and family routines. An educational label does not make a session unlimited or automatically beneficial. Ask a qualified health professional for individual health advice.

Try asking, "What are we using this for, and when will we stop?" A clear answer is more useful than treating every activity as interchangeable.

Choose an Activity, Not Just an App

Look at a few questions together before leaving a child with a learning tool. Can they understand the instructions? Is the challenge comfortable? Does a mistake lead to useful feedback? Are there ads, store links, or requests for personal information an adult needs to manage?

In our games, timed races and battles are different from guided lessons and untimed practice. If a countdown is frustrating, choose a table-practice activity without a timer. A lively screen is not necessarily a good fit for that moment.

Can a Learning App Replace Hands-On Practice?

No single app covers every learning need. Pair a screen activity with a paper clock, objects to count, drawing, reading, or a conversation. Points and streaks are not proof of understanding.

After a clock activity, ask your child to show a familiar time on a paper clock. After practising a multiplication fact, build that many equal groups with blocks. Our telling-time path and times-tables path offer places to start. A printable practice sheet is another option, not an extra task your child must complete.

What If Screen Use Feels Uncomfortable?

Pause the activity, check the device setup, and make room for breaks. If your child reports discomfort or you have concerns about vision, sleep, or wellbeing, speak with a qualified health professional. This article does not diagnose or treat health conditions.

Follow the current age and safety guidance for the particular device, especially for products such as VR headsets. We do not rank screens by medical risk or suggest that an outdoor activity cancels out unlimited screen use.

Plan Stopping Points Together

Before starting, agree on a manageable finish: one round, one lesson, or a short period that suits your family. Give a reminder before it ends. If the activity is causing frustration, it is fine to stop sooner rather than finish a score or streak.

Keep room in the day for play, conversation, movement, and rest. Meals or another regular family moment can be a useful screen-free anchor if that works for you. Adults can join the routine too; it need not be a rule aimed only at children.

Notice, Adjust, and Keep Talking

A routine is something you can revisit. Ask what felt interesting, confusing, or too fast. Choose a different activity when needed. The aim is a workable family rhythm, not a perfect score for either parent or child.
