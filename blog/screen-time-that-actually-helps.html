Screen Time Routines: Choosing, Pausing and Stopping Well

A practical guide to the moments that cause friction: picking an activity, playing together, and ending a session. Not medical advice.

Screen-time arguments rarely start with the total number of minutes. They start at specific moments: choosing what to do, getting a child's attention, and ending the session. This guide is built around those moments, with example phrases and a checklist you can adapt.

These are suggestions from a studio that makes children's games, which gives us a view on the subject and a stake in it. They are not medical guidance, not a daily allowance, and not evidence of any learning result.

Different Uses, Different Questions

"Screen time" covers very different activities, and each raises a different question:

  • Watching: Is it a show you chose, or an autoplay chain? Autoplay is the point where many sessions run longer than planned.
  • Playing a skill game: Can the child explain what they are doing? Is there a natural place to stop, such as the end of a lesson or battle?
  • Creating: Drawing, writing or building a game usually has a visible product you can talk about together.
  • Connecting: A video call with a relative needs a plan for its end too, but the stopping conversation is different.

Naming the type of use before you start makes it easier to agree on what "finished" looks like.

A Routine to Try: Choose, Co-play, Stop

This is one structure to adapt, not a rulebook. Another household may need something different.

Step 1: Choose together, before the device is in hand

Offer two options: "Do you want to practise clocks or make something?" Choosing before the screen is on avoids bargaining once the child is absorbed.

Step 2: Agree the stopping point in advance

Pick something observable rather than a vague "soon": "until this lesson ends," "until the timer on the kitchen counter rings," or "two races." Say it once, clearly.

Step 3: Co-play for the first few minutes

Sit beside your child at the start. Ask them to tell you what the game is asking. If you are interested in an example, the clock-reading guide shows what a first lesson session might involve.

Step 4: Give a warning, then a clear ending

"Two more questions, then we save and stop." A concrete count is easier to accept than "five minutes."

Step 5: Ask one question afterwards

"What was the tricky one?" or "Show me what you made." Explaining a method tells you more than a score, and not wanting to talk right away is not a failure; try again later.

A game's energy counter or daily free battles are limits on that game's modes, not parental screen-time controls. Checking an App Before Your Child Uses It

Spend a few minutes with the app yourself first. Questions worth answering:

  • What does it ask for? A child's practice app should not need their full name, school or email to work.
  • What can a child reach alone? Look for settings, purchases, ads or external links. Note whether a gate exists and what it actually verifies.
  • How are ads shown? Check whether they interrupt play, and whether they are clearly separate from game elements.
  • What is free? Find out what is unlocked, and whether progress is lost if you pay nothing.
  • Where does a session end? A lesson, a level or a round gives you a natural stopping point. Endless feeds do not.
  • Where does progress live? Local saves, accounts and cloud sync have different privacy trade-offs.

Look at the Whole Day

Consider both content and total time, including schoolwork and entertainment. A useful app still needs to fit around rest, movement, conversation, and screen-free play. An educational label is not a reason to ignore a child's discomfort or tiredness.

For individual advice about sleep, eyesight, development, or wellbeing, speak with a qualified health professional. For general guidance, consult authoritative sources such as your national health service or paediatric association. This guide does not diagnose problems or prescribe a daily screen allowance.

When Stopping Is Hard

Resistance at the end of a session is common and does not mean you did something wrong. Things you can try:

  • Move the warning earlier and attach a count ("two more races") instead of a time.
  • Plan what comes next before the device goes off: a snack, a walk, a paper activity such as drawing a clock face and setting it to the time you just practised.
  • Save, then say so: "Your progress is saved on this device; we can come back tomorrow." (Our browser saves stay in that browser on that device.)
  • Review afterwards when everyone is calm, and change the plan for next time instead of arguing about the last one.

If the stopping moment is regularly very upsetting, or you are worried about sleep or wellbeing, talk with a health professional or your child's school rather than relying on a guide like this.

Our Limits

We have not measured learning improvement caused by our games, and we are not child-health experts. A game, a story, or a paper activity can each have a place. Use what is here as a starting list, adapt it to your family, and change it when it stops helping.
