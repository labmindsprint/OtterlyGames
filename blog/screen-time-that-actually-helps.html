Screen Time That Actually Helps: A Mom's Honest Guide

A mom who builds educational apps shares her honest approach to kids' screen time.

In this article

  1. Not All Screen Time Is Created Equal
  2. My Personal Screen Time Rules
  3. Red Flags in Kids' Apps
  4. Look at the Whole Day
  5. A Short Session as a Starting Point
  6. The Honest Truth

Here's my confession: I build educational apps for kids, and I still worry about screen time. Every time I hand my child a tablet, there's a tiny voice in my head asking, "Is this okay?"

Screens serve different purposes in family life. What helps me is thinking about how we use them, how long an activity lasts, and what else needs room in the day.

Here are some ideas from our family. They are personal choices, not medical guidance or a promise of learning results.

Not All Screen Time Is Created Equal

The biggest mistake in the screen time conversation is treating it as one thing. It's not. There's a massive difference between:

  • Passive consumption: Watching random YouTube videos endlessly
  • Active engagement: Solving math problems in a game, creating art, building in Minecraft
  • Social connection: Video calling grandparents, playing with friends
  • Creative production: Making videos, coding, writing stories

Lumping these together is like saying "all food is the same." A carrot and a candy bar are both food. They are not the same.

My Personal Screen Time Rules

These are examples from trial, error, and figuring out what works for our family. Another household may need a different routine.

Rule 1: Choose the Activity Together

We talk about whether we want to make something, practise a skill, watch a story, or call family. A learning game does not have to be a ticket to earn another activity. We choose a manageable task and agree on a stopping point.

Rule 2: The "What Did You Learn?" Test

After a session, I might ask what felt interesting or confusing. Explaining how they worked out 7×8 can tell me more than a score. Not wanting to talk immediately is not proof that a session failed; we can revisit it later.

Rule 3: No Screens During Three Times

  • During meals
  • One hour before bed
  • First 30 minutes after waking up

These boundaries are non-negotiable. Everything else is flexible.

Rule 4: Together Time Counts More

Sitting with my child while they play a learning game is completely different from handing them a device and walking away. Co-playing, asking questions, celebrating their wins — this transforms screen time from isolation to connection.

Rule 5: Built-In Limits Are Your Friend

Math Tank has three daily free battles, and Clock Master uses race fuel. These are limits on particular modes, not parental screen-time controls: other activities remain available, and the Android apps can offer optional rewarded videos for extra play. Browser videos are unavailable. We still agree on when to stop rather than relying on a game's energy counter.

Red Flags in Kids' Apps

Here are a few things I check before choosing an app:

  • Asking for personal information: A kids' app should never need your child's name, school, or email
  • No parental gate: If a child can access settings, payment, or external links without adult verification, that's a design choice — and it's the wrong one
  • Aggressive ads: Full-screen ads every 30 seconds, especially ones that look like game elements
  • "Free" with immediate paywall: Download is free, but everything is locked behind purchases
  • Infinite engagement: Apps designed to keep kids playing forever with no natural stopping points
  • External links: Any app that can send a child to YouTube, social media, or web browsers

What we do differently

Our games need no account and keep progress on the current device or browser. A math-question gate helps reduce accidental settings changes; it does not verify that someone is an adult. Browser games have no native ad rewards. The optional Android apps may show AdMob banners and optional rewarded videos when online. Website hosting and adult support messages involve other services, as explained in our privacy policy.

Look at the Whole Day

I consider both content and total time, including schoolwork and entertainment. A useful app still needs to fit around rest, movement, conversation, and screen-free play. An educational label is not a reason to ignore a child's discomfort or tiredness.

For individual advice about sleep, eyesight, development, or wellbeing, speak with a qualified health professional. This parent guide does not diagnose problems or prescribe a daily screen allowance.

A Short Session as a Starting Point

If we want to practise, I might suggest a short session, perhaps ten minutes, then check whether to finish sooner or try something different. That is an example, not an evidence-based dose that every child needs each day.

I look for signs such as:

  • The child stays focused and engaged
  • The child has time to think instead of rushing
  • We can discuss a method or revisit a mistake together
  • The activity fits our plans for the day

Progress varies. We have not measured a fixed improvement from using our games, and a score or streak is not a deadline for understanding. Ask your child's teacher about a suitable next step if an idea remains confusing.

The Honest Truth

I still have days where my son watches too much TV. I still sometimes use a tablet as a babysitter while I cook dinner. I'm not a perfect parent, and this isn't a perfect system.

What helps me is noticing how an activity is going and being willing to change it. A game, a story, or a paper activity can each have a place. Learning together can be otterly playful without every minute needing a result. 🦦
